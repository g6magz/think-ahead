# Think Ahead Investment Project — Full Rundown for Jason

Prepared November 25, 2026, America/Denver. Written to be read aloud. Every figure is marked as modeled from a named file, stated by Jason and still concept-stage, or missing. Nothing here is invented.

## 1. Executive summary and core concept

Jason, this is the full, source-verified rundown of the Think Ahead Investment Project, as of tonight, November 25, 2026, Denver time.

Think Ahead is described as a Colorado public-health investment program in the GitHub README and in this project's bot profile. The idea is to prevent impaired driving, cut homeowners insurance, and put hail-protection solar on houses, financed by Invest Ahead, a 0 percent loan meant to roll into the first mortgage. The profile also names an RTD overnight ride network from 10 p.m. to 6 a.m., an impaired-driver hazard protocol, HOV slow-lane fines, and a $100,000 DUI penalty. Those last items are stated in the profile only. I found no written protocol or cost model for them in the repo or the Drive folder.

The only fully written work is two business-plan drafts, both dated November 23, 2026, in the repo g6magz/think-ahead, under /workspace/think-ahead/business-plan/, and as Google Docs in the Think Ahead Drive folder.

Modeled in solar-shade-system.md, a typical 2,000 square-foot A-frame shade system is priced at $172,500, with a 35 percent gross margin of $60,400 and a 25.2 percent contribution margin of $43,420 after sales cost and a warranty reserve. The 30 percent federal home solar credit ended for installs finished after December 31, 2025, so none of these paybacks count on it.

Modeled in whole-home-solar-payback.md, the five-piece package — snow-melt roof, ground-length screens, garage roll-out shades, backyard gazebo, and front carport — is about 100 kilowatts and $725,500 at 30 percent efficiency, or about 110 kilowatts and $797,500 at 33 percent, making 117,830 or 129,640 kilowatt-hours a year. If every kilowatt-hour were worth 15 cents, simple payback is 41 years at both efficiencies. Under Xcel's real credit cap, it is about 278 years or 290 years. None of these systems pays back inside 10 years. The fastest piece on electricity alone is the front carport, at about 15 and a half years, and only if that power can be used at the retail rate.

A later hypothetical in that same file, section 15, sells all power at double retail, pays the homeowner's bill, and lets Think Ahead keep the spread. That is not current Colorado utility policy. If Think Ahead owns the system and buys no home battery, payback from the spread is about 21 years, or about 23 years after upkeep.

The program insurance target is a 10 percent cut each year for 10 years, "to about $1,100." Starting from the cited 2026 Colorado average premium of $5,511, the arithmetic lands at about $1,922, not $1,100. That is modeled in both drafts. Reaching $1,100 from today's average would need a steeper path, or a starting premium of about $3,155. There is no start date for year one. The repo and Drive folder were created November 23, 2026. The 10-year premium clock and the 18-year exit have not begun.

## 2. Every revenue stream and side business, with numbers

Here is every revenue idea that actually has a number, and what does not.

First, selling the shade system, as modeled above. The cost of funding the 0 percent loans is not modeled. The $172,500 price is about 5.4 times a regular 12-kilowatt rooftop system in Colorado, so the draft says to sell it as hail protection that also makes power.

Second, selling an optional homeowners policy. Modeled in solar-shade-system.md, section 10. Year-one commission is $744 to $827 per policy, then about 12 percent of the declining premium, down to about $231 by year 10. Colorado law says this cannot be required as a condition of the loan. On a placeholder ramp of 100 to 1,700 systems a year, with a 60 percent attach rate, 10-year commission revenue is about $4.91 million. That ramp is a planning placeholder, not a board-approved forecast.

Third, the double-retail energy spread, if Think Ahead owns the whole-home system. Modeled in whole-home-solar-payback.md, section 15. At 30 cents a kilowatt-hour, yearly revenue is $35,349 at 30 percent efficiency and $38,892 at 33 percent. The homeowner gets about $1,051 back for an average Xcel bill. Think Ahead keeps a spread of $34,298 or $37,841, and about $31,305 or $34,548 after solar upkeep. A small home battery pushes payback to about 25 years. A battery sized to three times daily production never pays back after upkeep. No Colorado utility pays double retail today. Xcel pays about 1.16 cents for leftover power. The whole case hangs on a real buyer contract or a new PUC-approved rate. If Think Ahead owns the system, the homeowner's 25-year benefit is about $102,900, modeled in section 15.4. That is $26,280 of electric bills paid, plus $76,647 of insurance savings as the premium falls from $5,511 to $1,922. Year one is worth about $1,602, rising to about $4,640 a year from year 10 on. A 9,000 kilowatt-hour home pushes the 25-year total to about $110,400. If the homeowner bought the package and only received the bill back, it would be a bad buy, on the order of 160 years or more.

Fourth, Jason's November 23 to 25 storage-network ideas, still concept-stage, with no site-count model yet. He said Think Ahead would be the data center, guarantee the power sale so the facility can run carbon-negative, offer sites first to prisons — that links to the separate Prison Data Center project, which has its own draft book — then to schools, sell surplus to the highest bidder, and let homeowners see and place sites in their own areas. He also named product tiers: buy now and invest in data centers; let us pay your electric bill; and own your own system and sell the power back to us. Those tiers have not been priced as a set.

The bot profile says there are 7 side businesses. I did not find those seven named, or any numbers for them, in the repo, the Drive folder, or the two plan files. That is missing. Hail blankets are named as a planned product. They have not been priced.

## 3. Ten-year projections and the 18-year exit range

There is no program-wide 10-year profit-and-loss, and no 18-year exit model, in the repo or the Drive folder. That is missing. What does exist is the placeholder ramp of 8,350 systems over 10 years, and about $4.91 million of insurance commissions, modeled as an illustration in solar-shade-system.md, section 10.6. The same file says Front Range housing is about 2.1 million units, and the 13 Hail Alley states together have about 39.8 million, so demand size is not the decade bottleneck. Year-10 labor, factory certification, close rates, and 0 percent loan capital are.

The bot profile states an 18-year exit of $750 million to $9 billion. I found no worksheet or valuation method behind that range. Treat it as stated in the profile only, concept-stage, and unverified.

A post-payback split was worked in the November 23 conversation and is not yet saved to the repo or Drive. After payback around year 23 with upkeep counted, the yearly net of about $31,305 or $34,548 becomes profit to split. If Think Ahead then also covers the remaining $1,922 insurance premium, the homeowner would get about $2,973 a year and Think Ahead would keep about $29,383 or $32,626, or about $53,500 and $529,000 to $587,000 over 18 years after payback, before panel wear-down of roughly half a percent a year. Treat those split totals as conversation modeling, not a saved plan exhibit.

## 5. Contractor network

There is no contractor-network roster, no signed sub list, and no crew playbook. What is modeled, in solar-shade-system.md section 14, is labor need on the placeholder ramp. At about one system per crew per week, year 1 needs about 8 technicians, year 5 about 56, and year 10 about 136. Colorado has about 620 solar installers. Year 10 would be a large share of that pool if all volume stayed in-state. The draft says the work can keep up only with training, multi-state crews, and maybe a second factory shift or line. That is a capacity estimate, not a named network.
