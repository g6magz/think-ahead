# Think Ahead — Solar Shade System: Pricing, Financing, and Insurance

**Date:** November 23, 2026 (America/Denver); sections 14–15 added the same day  
**Document type:** Business-plan section (research draft)  
**Market:** Denver / Front Range, Colorado, with later expansion to 13 Hail Alley states  
**Program context:** Think Ahead is a Colorado public-health investment program. Hail protection is planned to include hail blankets, autonomous solar shade screens, solar carports, and a total solar roof with snow-melt pulses. Financing is **Invest Ahead**, 0% APR, intended to be rolled into the first mortgage, combined with a credit-free in-house loan backed by grid / bill savings. Insurance premiums are a program target: a 10% reduction each year for 10 years, to about $1,100.

**How to read this draft.** Every dollar figure is either taken from a cited source or labeled as an **estimate**. Comparable products exist at patio and RV scale; a roof-scale, tilting, dual-plane solar shade for A-frame homes is not a catalog product, so installed cost, yield, and life are planning estimates until an engineer and a factory BOM exist. This is not a customer quote, an actuarial filing, or a licensed engineering opinion.

Headline takeaways:

- **Equipment analogs** for finished retractable solar awnings run about **$3.35–$14.48/W** before a whole-roof install (see section 3). Conventional fixed rooftop solar in Colorado averages **$2.64/W installed** (EnergySage, November 2026) — about **$31,700** for a 12 kW array. A dual-plane, motorized, tilting shade is a different product, and the base delivered-cost estimate is several times a standard solar quote.
- **The 30% federal residential credit (IRC section 25D) is not available** for systems whose installation is completed after December 31, 2025 (IRS; Public Law 119-21). Break-even cases below assume **no federal ITC**.
- **Xcel Energy Colorado net metering is mostly a bill credit, not cash.** Excess kWh bank month to month at retail, by time-of-use period. Year-end leftovers cash out at the utility’s Average Hourly Incremental Cost (AHIC), a supply-cost rate well below retail, unless the customer elects rollover. A loan “backed by grid sales” cannot treat Xcel as a cash offtaker.
- **Energy savings alone do not pay off the recommended $172,500 price** within a typical 25-year solar life. The program insurance path is the swing factor, and it is a **target**, not a current filed discount. Starting from a cited 2026 Colorado average premium of **$5,511**, a 10%/year reduction for 10 years lands at about **$1,922**, not $1,100. Reaching $1,100 would require a starting premium of about **$3,155**, or a steeper schedule.
- **Think Ahead cannot require the customer to buy its insurance** as a condition of Invest Ahead or the system loan (C.R.S. section 10-3-1105 and section 10-2-602). Insurance must stay optional.

## 5. Incentives, rates, and the value of a kilowatt-hour

### 5.1 Federal ITC — not available for 2026 installations

The Residential Clean Energy Credit equals 30% of qualified costs for property installed from 2022 **through December 31, 2025**. The IRS states: “The credit is not available for any property placed in service after December 31, 2025.” Public Law 119-21 (July 4, 2025) ended section 25D for **expenditures made after December 31, 2025**, and an expenditure is treated as made when **original installation is completed**. Paying in 2025 for a 2026 install does not qualify (IRS FAQ FS-2025-05, August 21, 2025). Unused credit from a 2025-or-earlier install can still be carried forward.

**All payback tables below assume $0 federal residential credit.** A later law could change this; do not plan on a restored credit.

Commercial or third-party owned systems may still have a separate credit path (for example section 48E, with its own expiration and rules). That is a different ownership model than a homeowner-owned Invest Ahead system and is not modeled here.

### 5.2 Colorado and local incentives

- **State sales-tax exemption** on components used to produce renewable electricity (modules, inverters, racks, wiring, controls, switchgear): C.R.S. section 39-26-724. Labor, batteries, and monitoring are generally **not** exempt. Many local taxes still apply unless the locality adopted the exemption ([Colorado Department of Revenue](https://tax.colorado.gov/sales-use-tax-topics-renewable-energy-components)).
- **Property-tax exemption** for independently owned residential solar is widely cited under C.R.S. section 39-3-102 (confirm with counsel; not re-read line-by-line for this draft).
- **Xcel Solar*Rewards** residential upfront rebates in 2026 are for **income-qualified customers and disproportionately impacted communities**, not for every Xcel home. A settlement approved in Xcel’s 2026–27 Renewable Energy Compliance Plan increased that incentive to **$3/W** for those customers (GRID Alternatives; pv magazine USA, April 9, 2026; confirm on [Xcel’s program page](https://co.my.xcelenergy.com/s/renewable/solarrewards-iqdic) before using it in a quote). **Base-case payback assumes $0 rebate.**
- Some cities (for example Fort Collins, Boulder) run their own rebates or grants. They do not apply to a typical Denver Xcel home.

### 5.3 Xcel Energy Colorado rates and net metering

Default residential service is **time-of-use** (customers may opt out). PUC-described **base** energy rates from November 2025, and all-in volumetric rates compiled from Xcel’s Colorado rate book as served November 15, 2026 (GreenCalculus, reviewed November 2026, before city fees):

| Period | Hours | All-in cents/kWh (ex-city fees) |
|---|---|---:|
| Summer (Jun–Sep) on-peak | 5–9 p.m., non-holiday weekdays | 36.37¢ |
| Summer off-peak | All other summer hours | 14.50¢ |
| Winter (Oct–May) on-peak | 5–9 p.m., non-holiday weekdays | 31.56¢ |
| Winter off-peak | All other winter hours | 12.72¢ |

The monthly service and facility charge is **$7.10** (not offset by solar). Denver’s franchise fee is commonly reported at **3%**. Solar produces almost entirely in off-peak (daylight) hours. On Schedule NM, off-peak exports do **not** cancel on-peak (evening) use. The right planning value for a kWh of shade-system output, without a battery, is therefore close to the **off-peak all-in rate**, not the bill-average that includes on-peak use and fixed charges.

Values used:

| Scenario | ¢/kWh | Reason |
|---|---:|---|
| Low | 12.72¢ | Winter off-peak all-in, no franchise fee |
| Base | **13.71¢** | Season-weighted off-peak (8/12 × 12.72¢ + 4/12 × 14.50¢ = 13.31¢) × 1.03 Denver franchise |
| High | 16.5¢ | Near an all-in flat or higher self-consumption or first-year rate level; EIA-like residential average was about 16.6–16.8¢ over the year to August 2026, but that average includes fixed charges |

**Schedule NM (Colorado):** excess kWh carry month to month and credit **1:1 against retail use in the same TOU period**. Within 60 days after calendar year-end, leftover kWh are cashed out at **AHIC** unless the customer has elected the rollover option (no cash at move-out under rollover). A secondary compilation reported a 2025 AHIC of about 1.16¢/kWh; **this draft did not independently verify the official 2025 or 2026 AHIC from Xcel’s tariff.** Treat year-end cash as **much less than retail**, and do not build loan underwriting on a cash buyback.

## 6. Recommended customer pricing and margins

Gross margin here is **(price − delivered cost) / price**, where delivered cost is factory cost plus subcontracted installation (the cash it takes to put a system on a house), before customer-acquisition cost, warranty reserve, and cost of funds.

On the **base delivered cost of $112,100**:

| Gross margin | Price | $/W (12 kW active) | $/sq ft of roof | Margin $ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 25% | $149,500 | $12.46/W | $74.73 | $37,400 |
| **35% (recommended target)** | **$172,500** | **$14.37/W** | **$86.23** | **$60,400** |
| 45% | $203,800 | $16.98/W | $101.91 | $91,700 |

Rounded recommended price: **$172,500** per typical system, or about **$14.40/W** of active capacity and **$86/sq ft** of roof.

**Why 35%, not 25% or 45%.**

- 25% is tighter than this product’s risk supports in early production: novel mechanics, short analog warranties (5–10 years), and high likely callback and engineering costs.
- 45% would push a system that is already several times the price of fixed solar even further from a comparable-looking quote, unless insurance savings are guaranteed — and they cannot be, today.
- 35% is a standard hardware-plus-install target. On the base price it leaves about **$60,400** of gross margin. After a cited-scale solar CAC of **$0.84/W** in 2026 (SurgePV), or about **$10,100** on 12 kW, and a **4% warranty reserve** (estimate, ~$6,900) because this is not a 25-year crystalline module, roughly **$43,000** remains before other SG&A and the cost of funding 0% credit. That is a planning cushion, not a proven profit.

**Competitive comparison.** EnergySage’s Colorado installed average of $2.64/W implies about **$31,700** for 12 kW of ordinary rooftop solar. The recommended shade-system price is about **5.4 times** that figure; even the base delivered cost is about **3.5 times**. Think Ahead should not sell this as “solar at a solar price.” It is a **hail-protection and roof-shading system that also generates power**. Energy savings are a contributor, not the standalone justification, until factory cost moves toward the low case (35% margin on the low delivered cost would be about **$88,500**, still 2.8 times a 12 kW fixed array, with a much shorter payoff — about **17 years** in the base energy case with the insurance bundle).

**Cost-down is a requirement, not a nicety.** If delivered cost cannot fall from about $112,000 toward the $50,000–$90,000 band, the $172,500 list price will be a hard sale next to fixed solar plus a Class 4 or FORTIFIED reroof.

## 8. Merged financing: Invest Ahead 0% plus a credit-free, grid-backed in-house loan

Jason’s direction is to treat these as **one structure**, not two unrelated products.

### 8.1 How the two layers work together

1. **Invest Ahead (0% APR, rolled into the first mortgage).** The system price (about $172,500 in the recommended case) is financed at 0% interest and, when a mortgage refinance or purchase allows it, added to the first-lien mortgage. Monthly cash due for this layer is principal only, amortized with the mortgage. This layer is the long-term, lower-risk capital, but it is **not credit-free** in practice: first-mortgage lenders underwrite credit, income, appraisal, and title. Ability-to-repay and qualified-mortgage rules still apply to the mortgage even if the program sets the solar increment at 0%.

2. **Credit-free in-house loan, no credit check, “backed” by grid / bill savings.** This layer is meant to qualify households the mortgage process would delay or decline, or to bridge the period before a mortgage roll-in closes, or to hold any balance the mortgage cannot absorb. There is no interest. The intended repayment source is the household’s **electric-bill reduction and any net-metering credits**, plus — if the customer opts in — insurance-premium savings (section 9).

**Who qualifies.** Program intent: broad access in the Denver / Front Range base market, without a credit score gate on the in-house layer. A practical split: any owner-occupied Xcel (or later, other Hail Alley utility) site that can host the system; the mortgage-roll layer additionally requires a willing first-lien lender and a supportable loan-to-value after the roll-in. Income-qualified / DIC households may also stack Solar*Rewards if they meet Xcel’s rules — that is separate from loan underwriting.

**How “grid revenue” services the loan.** Under Schedule NM, Xcel almost always pays in **account credits**, not a monthly check. The lender cannot casually assign those credits. A workable servicing design is: (a) estimate annual bill savings (base about $2,286); (b) collect an **ACH or on-bill payment** from the customer in that amount; (c) true up once a year against actual Xcel statements; (d) apply 100% of receipts to **principal** at 0%. Year-end AHIC cash, if any, is small and should be treated as a residual, not as underwriting cash flow. Saying the loan is “backed by grid sales” is accurate only as a **customer cash-flow** story, not as a utility payable to Think Ahead.

**Mortgage-roll option.** When a purchase or refinance closes, the in-house balance is paid off (or assigned) and replaced by a 0% increment on the first mortgage. Until then, Think Ahead or a warehouse partner carries the in-house note.

**Default and transfer on sale.** Colorado solar-finance law (C.R.S. section 6-1-1805, from SB25-299, effective for covered agreements on or after July 1, 2026) requires clear term-and-cost disclosure, an explanation of any encumbrance on the real property, and notice of any UCC filing against the system. Recommended handling: the system is a **fixture**; the in-house loan is secured by a UCC fixture filing and, after roll-in, by the first deed of trust; on sale, the buyer **assumes** the remaining 0% balance or the seller pays it off at closing. Due-on-sale without an assumption path would strand a novel system. Removal of the system on default is legally possible if it is only personal property, and is a poor outcome for the roof and the lender.

**Lender capital.** At $172,500 and 100 systems in year 1, a fully in-house book is about **$17 million** of 0% receivables, before any mortgage take-out. That requires a warehouse line, a bank partner, or public Invest Ahead capital. Because the in-house layer skips credit checks, loss-given-default and required reserves will be higher than a prime solar loan book. If the mortgage partner takes out most loans at closing, Think Ahead needs mainly **bridge capital**. Someone must also fund the interest subsidy implicit in 0% (for example, if warehouse funds cost 6–8%, the subsidy on a long amortization is large). That subsidy should be budgeted as a program expense.

### 8.2 Regulatory issues (not legal advice; counsel needs to confirm)

- **Colorado Uniform Consumer Credit Code.** Nonbank consumer lending generally requires a **supervised-lender license** (C.R.S. Title 5; [Colorado Attorney General, UCCC licensing](https://coag.gov/licensing/uniform-consumer-credit-code/)). First-lien residential mortgages are largely outside UCCC rate caps, but **disclosure and some remedies still apply**. A 0% credit-free installment loan that is *not* a first mortgage is very likely UCCC-covered.
- **TILA / Regulation Z.** Colorado C.R.S. section 5-3-101 requires TILA and Consumer Leasing Act disclosures on consumer credit transactions even when a federal exemption might otherwise apply. APR, amount financed, payment schedule, and security interest must be clear — including an APR of 0%.
- **SB25-299 / C.R.S. Title 6, Article 1, Part 18.** Extra solar-finance disclosures, including UCC and real-property encumbrance effects.
- **Xcel payment form.** Bill credits, not cash, as above. Any marketing that implies a “utility paycheck” would be misleading.
- **No-credit-check vs. mortgage QM.** These two layers are in tension. Do not advertise a first-mortgage roll-in as “no credit check.”
- **Insurance tying.** See section 10.5. The loan and the policy must be separable.

### 10.4 Hail-mitigation discounts: what the market actually does

Colorado does **not** mandate a FORTIFIED or Class 4 dollar credit. Each insurer files its own.

- Industry summaries of Colorado Class 4 (UL 2218) credits commonly cite about **5–25% or 5–30%**, often applied only to the **wind-and-hail portion** of the premium, not the whole policy (Colorado Roofing Association range of “about 5% to 25%,” as reported in discussions of SB26-155; contractor compilations of 2026 renewals describe carrier-by-carrier bands, for example State Farm often 20–30% of the wind-and-hail part). Those contractor-observed bands are **not** official filings; use them as a range, and verify on a specimen policy.
- Denver County: hail is about **49.8%** of the homeowners premium (Colorado Division of Insurance, as cited above). A **15%** credit on that portion of a $5,511 premium is about **$412/year**. A 5–30% credit on that portion is about **$137–$823/year**.
- **IBHS FORTIFIED** certifies the **roof system** (sealed deck, enhanced nailing, edge metal, and, for the hail supplement, impact-rated covering), not just the shingle. SB26-155 (signed June 4, 2026, effective August 12, 2026) defines a “resilient roof system” as FORTIFIED or a similar science-based certification the board approves, creates the Strengthen Colorado Homes Enterprise, and, no sooner than January 1, 2027, requires insurers to **report** discounts and claim results for resilient-roof homes (C.R.S. section 10-4-405(1.2)). It does **not** by itself require a 10%/year credit.
- A retractable hail shade is **not** automatically a Class 4 roof or a FORTIFIED designation. Any Think Ahead discount must be separately filed and justified (hail-mode stow, third-party tests, claims experience). Do not advertise Class 4 or FORTIFIED credits unless the underlying roof actually qualifies.

**The 10%/year for 10 years path is more aggressive than current mitigation credits** and would need actuarial support, DOI approval, and a carrier (or captive) with appetite. Carrier appetite for Front Range hail is already tight; a guaranteed declining premium is a large promise.

### 10.5 Anti-rebating and anti-tying (Colorado)

- **Favored agent / coercion of debtors — C.R.S. section 10-3-1105.** No one may require, as a condition of a loan, credit, or lease, that the borrower take insurance through a particular insurer, agent, or broker. A lender also may not unreasonably reject a borrower’s own policy if it meets uniformly applied coverage and solvency standards.
- **Financial-institution tying — C.R.S. sections 10-2-602 and 10-2-606.** Credit, property, or services may not be conditioned on buying insurance from that institution or a specified person. A customer must not be told, or led to believe, that Think Ahead insurance is required to get Invest Ahead or the shade system.
- **Rebating — C.R.S. section 10-3-1104(1)(g).** Any inducement not stated in the insurance contract (a rebate of premium, a special favor, or “anything of value”) is generally prohibited. A 10%/year credit must live **inside a filed policy or rating plan**, not as a side discount for buying the shade system. Limited non-cash gifts are carved out in section 10-3-1104(2.6) with cap and non-discrimination rules.
- **Insurance as inducement — section 10-3-1104(2.7).** An insurer or producer shall not offer insurance as an inducement to buy another policy, or advertise it as “free.”

**Practical rule for sales and for this loan design:** offer the policy, explain any filed mitigation credit, and illustrate how premium savings **could** be applied to the 0% principal. Do not make the policy, or the 10% path, a condition of the loan or the system price. Do not advertise a discount that is not in a filed rate.

## 11. A-frame roof design

This section records **Jason’s concept**, written faithfully. It is a design intent, not a stamped drawing. Nothing here is a claim that the idea is patented or patentable.

### 11.1 The mechanism

On an A-frame (gable) roof, each plane has a retractable, solar-collecting shade.

- In **collect mode**, the shade on one side **contracts and rises high enough** that it no longer shades the opposite roof plane. The **exposed** plane — the one now in the sun — **tilts to at least flat, or to 45 degrees**, so the collecting surface can be aimed for production rather than left at the roof’s native pitch.
- The opposite shade is the one that has been pulled up and out of the way. The two sides can switch which plane is exposed, so an east–west roof can follow the sun in a coarse way, and a north–south roof can keep the south plane exposed for most of the day.
- In **hail mode**, the shades **stow or deploy as a protective cover** over the roof (and, if designed for it, openings and equipment), rather than remaining in a collecting tilt that would expose the roof or present a large hail and wind target.
- **Snow-melt** is intended to work with the same platform: shades can retract so sun and any roof snow-melt pulses can reach the roof, or the electrical system can supply melt pulses, without leaving a snow-loaded collecting surface in an unsafe position. The control logic for “collect vs. stow vs. melt” needs a weather feed (hail, wind, snow load) and fail-safe retraction.

### 11.2 Why 45° (or flat) matters in Denver

Denver sits at about **39.7°N**. For a fixed south-facing array, the rule-of-thumb tilt is close to latitude, often about **35–40°**. PVWatts-type runs for Denver at latitude tilt, south, 14% system losses, are about **1,635 kWh/kW/year**. A 25° (about 6/12) south roof is reported around **1,626 kWh/kW/year** — almost the same. **45°** is a little steeper than latitude and is still in the near-optimal band for annual energy, slightly better in winter, slightly worse in summer. **Flat (0°)** is a real concession, especially in winter when the sun is low; it should be a stow or a transitional geometry, not the all-year collecting angle, unless engineering forces it. The production estimates in section 2 assume the base system spends most collecting hours near 45° (or latitude), not flat.

### 11.3 Hail-mode stow and snow-melt interplay

Hail in the Front Range can be frequent and severe (IBHS has documented higher stone counts and higher impact energy at elevation; a 1-inch stone in Denver can carry the energy of a larger stone at lower elevation). The control system has to retract or cover **faster than storm arrival**, including at night and when the homeowner is away, and it has to do so in rising wind. Solar Markise’s patio product is only rated to Beaufort 5 when extended — a warning that a much larger, higher, tilting surface will have a stricter wind limit and must stow early. Snow adds weight and can block retraction; a melt pulse or a “do not deploy” interlock is part of the same safety case. A failed stow in a hailstorm is both a damage event and an insurance-credit event (the credit depends on the shade actually working).

### 11.4 Engineering and other risks

- **Wind load and uplift.** A plane that tilts off the roof changes from a roof-following surface to a spoiler. Uplift, oscillation, and attachment into rafters or a ridge structure are first-order structural problems. Denver (and Front Range wind) will set the stow threshold.
- **Weight and the existing roof.** Motors, frames, and fabric on both planes, plus concentrated loads when one side is “risen high,” may exceed what an existing truss roof can take without reinforcement.
- **Electrical safety.** Moving PV conductors, flex-cycle fatigue, rapid-shutdown and NEC 690/705 compliance, and grounding of a moving array.
- **Permitting.** Above 10 kW, Denver requires PE-stamped plans. A moving structure may also need structural and, in landmark districts, design review. HOA covenants often restrict roof appearance, mechanical noise, and “awnings.”
- **Fail-safe and maintenance.** Power loss, ice, debris, and motor failure cannot leave the system stuck in a high-drag or roof-exposed position. Analog products quote short service lives; cycle life of the fabric and hinges is unknown at this scale.
- **Production vs. protection tradeoff.** Time spent stowed is time not generating. The 15% base availability haircut may be optimistic in hail season and windy spring weather.
- **Insurance recognition.** Until a carrier files a credit for *this* system, the homeowner should not be promised a Class 4 or FORTIFIED discount on the strength of the shade alone.

### 11.5 What still needs an engineer (and others) to verify

A Colorado-licensed structural and electrical engineer, and likely a wind-tunnel or ASCE 7 analysis, must still verify: attachments and load path; tilt and “rise high” kinematics and clearances; stow time and wind/hail sensors; snow and ice; hail-impact performance of the fabric and of the roof when covered; electrical flex-cycle and fire access pathways (Denver IRC-based 36-inch pathways and ridge setbacks still apply in spirit, even if the array moves); HOA and landmark constraints; and whether PE-stamped permit drawings can be standardized or must be site-specific. A patent search is a separate legal task; **this document makes no patentability claim.**

## 14. Scale capacity analysis

Question: can in-house manufacturing, subcontracted install labor, and autonomous sales keep up with demand? Demand is not the binding constraint in the first decade if the placeholder volume ramp in section 10.6 is the plan (100 systems in year 1, rising to 1,700 in year 10). Capacity and process are. The verdict at the end of this section is **yes, with conditions**.

### 14.1 Per-unit margins at the $172,500 price (explicit)

These figures use the **rounded recommended price of $172,500** and the base delivered cost and below-the-line items from sections 6 and 7. Gross margin is price minus delivered cost (factory cost plus subcontracted install). Contribution margin here subtracts customer-acquisition cost and the 4% warranty reserve as well. It does **not** subtract the unmodeled cost of funding 0% credit; that subsidy sits with Invest Ahead or a warehouse lender (section 8).

| | Dollars | Percent of $172,500 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Recommended price | $172,500 | 100% |
| Delivered cost (factory + install) | $112,100 | 65.0% |
| **Gross margin** | **$60,400** | **35.0%** |
| Customer acquisition ($0.84/W × 12 kW) | $10,080 | 5.8% |
| Warranty reserve (4% of price, estimate) | $6,900 | 4.0% |
| **Contribution margin** | **$43,420** | **25.2%** |

If CAC and warranty run as budgeted, about **$43,400 per system** remains to cover residual SG&A, the cost of 0% funds, and net profit. That contribution is an estimate, not a demonstrated result.

### 14.2 Addressable demand

**Denver / Front Range (base market).** The Colorado State Demography Office’s Vintage 2024 county estimates (prepared August 2025) count **2,074,191 housing units** in a 10-county Front Range core: Adams 203,379; Arapahoe 274,271; Boulder 147,925; Broomfield 34,581; Denver 378,170; Douglas 153,240; El Paso 317,276; Jefferson 258,400; Larimer 169,132; and Weld 137,817. Adding Pueblo, Elbert, and Teller (sometimes included in a broader corridor) raises the total to **2,174,386**. Statewide, the same file counts 2,678,831 housing units in 2024; the Census Bureau’s later July 1, 2025 estimate is **2,706,544** housing units in Colorado and **375,287** in Denver County.

The Denver-Aurora-Centennial metropolitan statistical area, a narrower geography than the 10-county corridor, is reported at **1,290,063 housing units** in the 2020–2024 ACS, of which **736,902 (57.1%)** were single-family detached (compilations of Census tables B25001 and B25024). City of Denver itself is more multifamily (owner-occupied rate 48.8% in the 2020–2024 ACS, Census QuickFacts), so the attachable market is concentrated in the suburban counties.

**Roof form.** Jason’s mechanism is for **A-frame / gable** geometry. A HUD review of U.S. home-building methods reported that **63%** of new homes in a late-1990s / 2000 dataset had **gable** roofs and 36% had hip roofs (HUD USER, *Review of Structural Materials and Methods for Home Building in the United States: 1900 to 2000*). That is new-construction, national, and dated; it is the best official-adjacent split found for this draft. Industry explainers often say gable roofs are more than half of U.S. roofs. True “A-frame” houses (steep, triangular cabins) are a **niche** and no reliable nationwide share was found; the product should be sized to **gable roofs**, not only vernacular A-frames. A 60%-range gable share applied to Front Range single-family detached stock is a **planning filter**, not a measured Front Range count.

**Solar adoption (already-solar homes are not automatically out, but they change the pitch).** SEIA’s Colorado state spotlight (November 2026) reports that **9.60% of Colorado homes have solar**. Public Service Company of Colorado (Xcel) had **121,127** residential net-metered installations at year-end 2025 (EIA-861, as compiled in November 2026). Those homes already have a production story; the shade system’s incremental case is hail protection and, if the existing array is displaced or shaded, a redesign.

**Order-of-magnitude addressable set (illustrative).** Start with 2.07 million Front Range housing units. If roughly half to three-fifths are single-family detached (the metro 57.1% is a cited floor; suburban counties will be higher) and roughly 60% of those have gable geometry (HUD-based filter, estimate), the geometric pool is on the order of **0.7–0.9 million roofs** before owner-occupancy, HOA, structural, historic-district, and willingness-to-buy screens. Even a severe cut to 10% of that pool leaves tens of thousands of roofs. The section 10.6 placeholder ramp of 100 to 1,700 systems a year is **well under 1%** of that pool. **Demand size is not the year-1-to-10 bottleneck** if the product and financing work.

**Thirteen Hail Alley states (expansion).** This draft uses the 13-state “hail belt” list from a Moody’s insurance-industry hail study: **Arkansas, Colorado, Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Kansas, Minnesota, Missouri, Nebraska, Oklahoma, South Dakota, Texas, and Wisconsin**. NOAA’s narrower geographic “Hail Alley” is the area where Nebraska, Colorado, and Wyoming meet; Wyoming is in that geography but **not** in the 13-state list, and only part of Texas is high-hail. Census Bureau housing-unit estimates, July 1, 2025 (V2025):

It is still orders of magnitude above a 1,700-per-year run rate. Expansion demand is not the constraint; **multi-state labor, permits, utilities, and 0% capital** are.

### 14.3 Manufacturing throughput, capex, and ramp

Section 7 used a **planning assumption** of a roughly **$4 million** pilot line amortized over about 1,000 systems ($4,000 per system). That is not a vendor quote.

Each typical system uses **24 kW of fabric** (~2,000 sq ft). Required annual fabric output on the placeholder ramp:

| Year | Systems (placeholder) | Fabric output | Active MW installed |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 100 | 2.4 MW (~200,000 sq ft) | 1.2 MW |
| 3 | 350 | 8.4 MW | 4.2 MW |
| 5 | 700 | 16.8 MW | 8.4 MW |
| 10 | 1,700 | 40.8 MW (~3.4 million sq ft) | 20.4 MW |

A 40 MW/year fabric line is **small** next to gigawatt-scale crystalline module plants. If the pilot line is built, certified (UL / IEC for a moving PV product), and yields are acceptable, **in-house manufacturing can keep up with this ramp**, perhaps with a second shift or a modest line expansion by year 10. The manufacturing risks are **schedule and certification**, not nameplate throughput: a late UL listing or poor early yield would idle install crews regardless of capex. Roll-to-roll thin film, if chosen instead of laminated crystalline fabric, is a different and usually heavier capex path and is not bid here.

### 14.4 Subcontracted install labor

A typical U.S. **standard rooftop** solar crew completes about **3–5 installs per week** (on the order of one ordinary system per day; highly standardized work can be faster; Tesla has published more aggressive two-per-day targets that do not apply to a custom moving roof). This shade system combines electrical work, a large retractable structure, possible roof reinforcement, and a PE-stamped permit over 10 kW. **Estimate:** **0.5–1 completed system per crew per week** (one to two weeks on site, plus weather and inspection slack). That is an estimate, not a time-and-motion study.

At **one system per crew-week** and about 50 productive weeks:

| Year | Annual volume | Crew-weeks needed | Standing crews | Technicians at 4 per crew |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 100 | 100 | ~2 | ~8 |
| 3 | 350 | 350 | ~7 | ~28 |
| 5 | 700 | 700 | ~14 | ~56 |
| 10 | 1,700 | 1,700 | ~34 | ~136 |

**Colorado labor availability.** BLS / state compilations of the May 2025 OEWS count about **620 solar photovoltaic installer** jobs in Colorado (SOC 47-2231), median pay about $49,730. O*NET / state projections put employment at 650 in 2024 and 900 in 2034 (~90 openings a year). SEIA (November 2026) reports **8,071 solar jobs** in Colorado more broadly (install, development, manufacturing, other) and 379 solar-and-storage companies. The Colorado Energy Office’s 2025 climate-workforce work flags solar panel installers among occupations with **large projected workforce gaps**. Electricians (SOC 47-2111, Colorado mean $31.88/hour in May 2025) are a second scarce input.

Year 1–3 crew needs (8–28 technicians) are small next to 620 PV installers **if** Think Ahead can hire or subcontract people and train them on a novel system. Year 10’s ~136 technicians would be on the order of **one-fifth of the state’s current PV-installer employment** if all volume stayed in Colorado — a real squeeze, even before ordinary solar employers compete for the same people. The fix is early training partnerships, multi-state crews as Hail Alley expansion starts, and a simpler install (for example PV on one plane only) if site time stays above one week.

### 14.5 Autonomous sales funnel and CAC

“Autonomous sales” is taken here to mean a software-led funnel (qualification, design, proposal, financing illustration) with a thin human closer network, not a large commissioned door-to-door force.

Cited CAC: Wood Mackenzie (March 25, 2026) reported U.S. residential solar CAC at a five-year low of **$0.60/W in 2025**, then a projected **40% rise to $0.84/W in 2026** after the §25D sunset and a contracting market. That $0.84/W is the figure already used in section 7 (**$10,080** per 12 kW system). Post-ITC, Wood Mackenzie also notes a shift toward in-house, multi-product acquisition; a $172,500 hail-plus-solar product is a harder close than a $30,000 solar array, so **autonomous channels may not actually achieve $0.84/W** until the product is familiar. Treat $0.84/W as a **solar-industry analog**, not a measured Think Ahead CAC.

Funnel math (close rate is an **estimate**). Ordinary residential solar close rates on qualified leads are often cited in the mid-single to low-double digits. For a novel, high-ticket system, this draft uses a **5% close rate on qualified proposals** as a planning case:

| Year | Systems | Qualified proposals at a 5% close | Implied annual CAC budget at $10,080 |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 100 | 2,000 | $1.0 million |
| 3 | 350 | 7,000 | $3.5 million |
| 5 | 700 | 14,000 | $7.1 million |
| 10 | 1,700 | 34,000 | $17.1 million |

Front Range housing counts are large enough to supply that lead volume. The risk is **conversion**, not raw names: HOA rules, a price several times fixed solar, a 0% financing story that must be told accurately (bill credits are not a utility paycheck; insurance cannot be tied), and trust in an unproven mechanism. If the close rate is 2% instead of 5%, proposal throughput and CAC both get much worse. The fix is a tighter offer (insurance illustration only when a filed credit exists; cost-down; optional human closers in year 1–3) and measurement of actual CAC before scaling ad spend.

### 14.6 Bottlenecks and fixes by year

| Year | Volume | Bottleneck | Fix |
|---:|---:|---|---|
| **1** | 100 | **Productization and sales**, not demand or line speed: UL / PE drawings, first-article yield, a trained install method, and a close rate on a $172,500 novel product. Autonomous sales is unproven at this price. | Finish certification before promising volume; two trained crews and a human-assisted close team; do not hire a 40 MW factory before yield is known. |
| **3** | 350 | **Trained specialty install crews** and permit-cycle time (PE stamp, possible structural work, HOA). Factory output of 8 MW/year should be manageable if the pilot line is up. | Apprenticeship / subcontractor academy; standardized site kits; pre-approved typical drawings with Denver and suburban jurisdictions. |
| **5** | 700 | **Install labor** in Colorado (14 standing crews) and **CAC** if close rates stay weak after the ITC sunset (Wood Mackenzie’s $0.84/W environment). 0% financing warehouse capacity also starts to matter. | Multi-county and then multi-state subs; measure CAC and cut poor channels; mortgage take-out so Think Ahead is not carrying every note. |
| **10** | 1,700 | **Labor and logistics in an expanded footprint**, plus a **factory increment** if still on a single pilot line (~41 MW/year). ~136 technicians would be a large claim on Colorado’s ~620 PV installers if volume had not already left the state. Capital for a large 0% book. | Second shift or a second line; Hail Alley crew bases; keep manufacturing ahead of sales by 6–12 months; public or bank capital for Invest Ahead. |

### 14.7 Verdict

**Yes, with conditions.** Addressable Front Range and Hail Alley demand is large enough that a 100-to-1,700 system ramp does not run out of roofs. In-house manufacturing can match that ramp **if** a certified line is actually built and yields. Subcontracted labor can match years 1–3 in Colorado; by years 5–10, labor — not demand — is the likely constraint unless training and multi-state expansion are underway. Autonomous sales can supply the funnel **if** close rates and CAC stay near the $0.84/W analog; if they do not, sales becomes the bottleneck earlier than the factory. Additional conditions: Invest Ahead capital for 0% loans, PE/permit standardization, and no plan that depends on tying insurance to the loan (section 10.5).

Shares below are labeled with the source’s own categories.

- **Permitting, inspection, and interconnection (PII):** NREL’s Q1 2017 detailed benchmark included **$0.10/Wdc** for PII (Fu / *Cost-Reduction Roadmap*, NREL/TP-6A20-70748). That is only about **3%** of the 2024 $3.25 total if the dollar amount were unchanged; Denver’s current **$50** PV building-permit fee is even smaller in dollars, but engineering, interconnection, and inspections still add time and cost. A planning range of **about 2–6%** of installed price is consistent with those sources; it is not a 2024 NREL line item.
- **Overhead / G&A:** The 2017 NREL benchmark used **$0.31/Wdc** for general and administrative overhead. Ramasamy et al. (2022) treated overhead as a **$2,389** fixed cost per project (about $0.30/W on an 8 kW system). On a $3.25/W MMP, that is on the order of **8–11%**.
- **Profit:** NREL’s long-standing modeling convention is a **17% margin on direct costs** (hardware, labor, sales tax, installation, and permitting fees; excluding CAC and overhead) in the MMP. In the 2017 table that profit line was **$0.32/Wdc**. As a share of today’s lower hardware-heavy or lower-price quotes, profit often lands around **8–12% of price**, but it varies widely by installer. LBNL (2026 data update) explicitly includes profit inside its soft-cost definition and does **not** publish a standalone profit share.

**LBNL Tracking the Sun / U.S. Distributed Solar and Storage 2026 data update (August 2026):** for host-owned stand-alone systems, **“soft costs and other BoS costs collectively represent roughly 80%”** of the median 2025 residential installed price. In that framing, the module and inverter indices are only about **20%**. Soft costs in LBNL’s definition include customer acquisition, installation labor, permitting and interconnection, some financing fees, taxes, and profit. This is a residual, not a full cost-accounting split.

**EnergySage Colorado (November 13, 2026):** average installed price **$2.64/W** ($30,076 for 11.4 kW). EnergySage does not publish a full installer P&L split in that market page.

**SEIA / Wood Mackenzie:** the Solar Market Insight series tracks volume and price; the Wood Mackenzie CAC outlook cited above is the detailed sales-cost benchmark used here. A full 2025–2026 SEIA/WoodMac installed-cost stack with labor and profit shares was not extracted as a standalone table for this draft.

### 15.2 Solar carports and canopies

NREL’s PV cost model is **not published as a carport-specific benchmark**. The extra cost versus rooftop is almost entirely **structure, foundations, engineering, and site work**.

A 2025 industry guide (SolarTechOnline, weaker than NREL/LBNL; use as an order-of-magnitude only) offered a representative per-watt stack: panels $0.50–$0.70, canopy structure $1.20–$1.50, inverters/electrical $0.30–$0.40, installation labor $0.60–$0.80, permits $0.10–$0.20, overhead $0.40–$0.60, implying a roughly **$3.10–$4.20/W** total. On the midpoint of that range (~$3.65/W), **structure-plus-modules-plus-electrical hardware is about 55–65%**, **labor about 16–22%**, **permits about 3–5%**, and **overhead about 11–16%**. Profit was not separately stated. Community carport planning guides (for example University of Michigan / CLCF 2025) likewise identify the canopy and civil work as the main premium over rooftop PV, without a national percentage table.

### 15.3 Retractable solar awnings

No NREL, LBNL, or SEIA/WoodMac **percentage cost-share table** was found for retractable solar awnings. What exists is price and component evidence from section 3 and section 4:

- Conventional motorized retractable awnings: about **$15–$30/sq ft** installed; national average around **$2,700** (Angi, October 31, 2026; Fixr, January 31, 2025).
- Installation labor on a whole awning often **$300–$1,500**; a motor-only electrician visit **$200–$800** (Awning Guide, October 2, 2026).
- Permit fees sometimes **$150–$250** (Angi).
- Finished solar-awning retail equipment: **$3.35–$14.48/W** (section 3).

**Derived estimate (not a published benchmark):** on a typical $2,700 motorized patio install, labor of $300–$1,500 is about **11–56%** (a mid-range $800 is about **30%**); equipment and materials are the majority, often **50–75%**; permits a few percent; sales, overhead, and profit are not broken out. Roof-scale Think Ahead work will not match a patio cassette, so this row is context only.

### 15.5 Comparison with Jason’s 25% labor / 15% material, and with our waterfall

**Jason’s 25/15 does not match current residential rooftop PV.** NREL’s Q1 2024 MMP puts **installation labor at about 6.5%** and **hardware at about 43%** — almost the reverse emphasis of 25% labor and 15% material. LBNL’s finding that ~80% of the 2025 median price is “soft costs and other BOS” can look labor-heavy if “soft” is mistaken for field labor; most of that 80% is CAC, overhead, profit, permitting, and non-module BOS, not wrench time. A 25/15 split resembles a conventional construction job, or an earlier solar era when modules were cheap relative to labor only in some local quotes, more than it resembles 2024–2026 national PV benchmarks.

**Against Think Ahead’s own base waterfall, 25/15 is closer, but only if “material” means PV fabric alone and “labor” means all labor.**

- **Materials:** PV-fabric materials are **16.7%** of the $172,500 price — close to Jason’s **15%**. But the customer is also paying for a moving structure, motors, and electrical BOS. All hardware is **35.7%**, more than twice 15%, and still a bit below NREL rooftop hardware’s 43% because our price is high relative to hardware (the 35% gross margin and $32,000 install dilute the hardware share).
- **Labor:** Field install is **18.6%** of price; factory plus field labor is **22.0%**. That is in the same band as Jason’s **25%**, and it is **much higher than NREL rooftop labor (6.5%)**, which is what one would expect for a retractable, tilting, roof-scale system.
- **What 25/15 leaves out:** at our price, CAC (5.8%), factory overhead and capex (7.2%), warranty (4%), and a 35% gross / 25% contribution margin are large. A 25+15=40% labor-and-material story accounts for less than half of the customer price. The rest is structure (if not counted as material), soft costs, and margin.

**Verdict on 25/15:** treat it as a **reasonable first cut for field labor and fabric materials on this product**, not as a description of standard rooftop solar, and not as a complete cost model. For planning, use the section 7 waterfall: about **36% hardware, 19% install labor, 7% factory overhead/capex, and 35% gross margin** (which itself funds CAC, warranty, and a ~25% contribution margin). If Jason’s 15% “material” was meant to be *all* hardware, it is too low for both NREL rooftop PV and this shade system.
